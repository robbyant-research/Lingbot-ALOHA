HALO combines kinesthetic teaching with a low-resistance mobility interface to collect coordinated whole-body demonstrations. Operators guide the robot end-effectors directly and move the collection platform through natural locomotion, with the mobile base removed. The platform preserves the deployed base's nonholonomic motion space. Deployment-aligned demonstration processing calibrates and temporally aligns platform motion with the base response and masks and inpaints demonstrator hands in recorded observations. Compared with Mobile ALOHA and VR teleoperation, HALO enables up to 2.9× faster demonstration collection with comparable or higher success rates. Learned policies perform Zip-Tie Fastening and achieve 73.3% success on a six-stage long-horizon mobile manipulation task.

How HALO Works

Leader–follower systems spatially separate the operator's hands, viewpoint and robot end-effectors, introducing a perception gap; the powered mobile base also constrains natural operator mobility, creating a mobility gap. HALO uses kinesthetic teaching to align the operator's perception–action space with the robot and removes the base during data collection to preserve natural locomotion. Deployment-aligned demonstration processing addresses the resulting visual and control discrepancies.

02 · Mobility interface

Mobility interface

Removing the powered mobile base preserves natural locomotion and reduces the horizontal force required for repositioning to 2.3 N. Natural body motion drives the low-resistance collection platform, enabling implicit base control during bimanual manipulation. Passive wheels and two fixed-direction wheels along the drive-wheel axis preserve the deployed base's nonholonomic motion space, without post-hoc retargeting.

03 · Manipulation interface

Kinesthetic end-effector redesign

HALO uses kinesthetic teaching to align the operator's perception and action space with the robot while preserving direct visual and force feedback. The end-effector removes ALOHA's handle-and-scissor mechanism and adopts a compact, high-contact design with increased hand contact area. The redesign reduces end-effector size by more than half and increases the available pitch angle by about 40°.

04 · Data processing

Deployment-Aligned Data Processing

Base motion alignment uses two encoders to measure collection-platform motion. Their readings are converted into left and right platform wheel velocities, uL and uR. An affine mapping converts the measured motion to deployment-compatible base linear and angular velocities, while temporal alignment accounts for the deployed base's response delay.

The coefficients and offsets are estimated from paired platform measurements and commanded base velocities. The estimated action–state delay is used for temporal alignment. Viewer readouts are illustrative.

Visual processing removes demonstrator-specific cues: CaRE-Ego detects the operator's hands, SAM2 propagates masks across frames, and DiffuEraser reconstructs the masked regions for observations that better match autonomous execution.

05 · Policy learning

Policy learning

The pretrained π0.5 vision-language-action model is fine-tuned on HALO demonstrations for each task. It receives one head-camera image, two wrist-camera images, robot joint positions, and base linear and angular velocities. The action vector concatenates arm actions with base linear and angular velocities. Asynchronous real-time action chunking (RTC) predicts the next action chunk while the current chunk is executed, conditioning on the committed action prefix to maintain continuity.

Collecting Demonstrations

The operator works with their own hands and walks at a natural pace. The robot's head camera sees those hands, so they are masked and inpainted afterwards, and the training images match what the robot sees on its own.

Implicit base control

The low-resistance collection platform allows base motion to arise naturally from the operator's movement, without explicit base control that could disrupt hand manipulation, thereby capturing coordinated mobile manipulation behaviors.

Efficiency against VR teleoperation and Mobile ALOHA

Same operator, same tasks, same time budget; the operator had not used any of the interfaces before. Counters in the video track the demonstrations collected with each interface on Push Chair (4× speed).

VR's perception gap makes precise gripper control hard; Mobile ALOHA's powered base constrains the operator, who pauses before grasping the chair.

Deployment-aligned demonstration processing

Deployment-aligned demonstration processing addresses visual and control discrepancies between demonstration and deployment. In visual processing, CaRE-Ego detects the operator's hands, SAM2 propagates masks across frames, and DiffuEraser reconstructs the masked regions; hand removal improves Push Chair success from 55% to 85%. Base motion alignment fits an affine mapping from platform motion to deployment-compatible base velocities and estimates the action–state delay for temporal alignment.

What the Policies Learn

Precise bimanual manipulation, coordinated base and arm motion over long horizons, and generalization beyond the layout seen during collection.

Generalization of mobile manipulation

Push Chair asks the robot to grasp a chair with both grippers while moving at about walking speed (~0.7 m/s) and push it under a table. The table never moved during data collection, yet the policy handles 2–3 m changes in its starting pose (Mobile ALOHA used 5–10 cm) and unseen environments. Velocity-based base actions favour a reactive pattern that keeps driving the chair toward the table rather than memorizing a global pose.

Dexterity

Zip-Tie Fastening chains picking, handover, folding, insertion, fastening and placement on a short, small zip tie. The HALO policy succeeds in 30% of 10 trials (41 s on average); policies trained on Mobile ALOHA and VR demonstrations achieve 0% success. In Disassemble Lego the policy succeeds 30 of 40 times, adapts its separation strategy to randomized structures, and generalizes to unseen configurations.

Long-horizon mobile manipulation

In the long-horizon mobile manipulation task, the robot retrieves a box from a shelf, loads objects into it, lifts it with both arms, drives to a conveyor, places it, and returns to repeat. Despite many forward, backward and turning base motions, the policy brings the base back to its starting pose so the loop can run again: 11 of 15 trials succeed (73.3%).

Quantitative Results

Policy success rates on real hardware, and the ablations from the paper. With the same collection budget, policies trained on Mobile ALOHA or VR data fail on Disassemble Lego and the long-horizon mobile manipulation task and score 0% on Zip-Tie Fastening.
